\caption{StereoSGBM: scene-average disparity MAE on valid predictions, and pixel-pooled coverage over fixed GT. The 15-pair rows include the two development scenes.}
\label{tab:stereo}

Table~\ref{tab:stereo} shows that moderate error on retained matches coexists with substantial missing output. Across all 15 pairs, only 2,873,146 of 4,586,010 GT pixels survive the prediction and consistency checks. Restricting to nonoccluded GT improves coverage, but does not eliminate missing matches. On the 13 held-out scenes, the supplementary mean conditional Z RMSE is 0.1201 m; it describes only surviving predictions and cannot replace the valid fraction.

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/stereo_jadeplant.png}
\caption{Jadeplant StereoSGBM diagnostics. Gray denotes unavailable values. Thin leaves, branches, and the foreground grid contain many rejected predictions. The mask-panel percentage divides by the entire image; evaluation coverage divides by valid GT. Display saturation does not clip saved predictions.}
\label{fig:stereo}
\end{figure*}
Figure~\ref{fig:stereo} illustrates the limitation on Jadeplant: valid-GT coverage is 37.39\%, with 0.1291 m conditional Z RMSE. Small branches and grid boundaries are difficult for local matching. Occlusion, weak/repeated texture, and windows crossing depth boundaries are plausible mechanisms, not individually isolated causal findings. PianoL similarly retains only 39.42\% of GT pixels, with 0.3594 m conditional RMSE. A surviving left--right check is therefore not proof that the match is correct. Through Eq.~\ref{eq:stereo}, small disparity errors can also cause larger depth errors when the disparity denominator is small.

All 654 predictions contribute to Table~\ref{tab:nyu}, covering 156,018,240 evaluated GT pixels. There are no nonfinite predictions in the evaluation region. Clipping affects 554,689 predictions above 10 m and none below 0.001 m. The aggregate improves relative to our historical unclipped score, but this is a protocol change, not a model improvement. We retain both versions and identify the current protocol explicitly.

The per-image distribution remains important. Figure~\ref{fig:nyu} uses a deterministic median/maximum selection rule rather than choosing only visually pleasing examples. The maximum-RMSE image has AbsRel 0.6198 and $\delta_1=0.2691$, despite much stronger aggregate scores. This shows a substantial individual failure under the same crop, clipping, and weighting. Filled GT is the supplied benchmark reference and may itself smooth missing sensor measurements; the experiment does not establish exact physical geometry at every visible boundary.

Raw DA's development $T$ is 0.005168. Increasing the history weight lowers the change proxy but increases departure from the current prediction (Table~\ref{tab:selection}). Contact sheets at frames 15, 45, 75, and 99 show that E4 remains close to the moving silhouette; differences among the three E4 candidates are visually small. We choose the intermediate 0.70 as a conservative balance, not a proven optimum. The review and all candidate recomputations precede test inference and fusion. No parameter is changed after inspecting the rollercoaster results.

\label{tab:video}

All methods have 100\% pair coverage on the same 14,180,855 test reference pixels over 69 pairs; the development denominator is 22,391,812 over 99 pairs. Thus the reductions do not arise from dropping more evaluated pairs. They apply only within RGB/flow-supported regions, which omit unreliable correspondences and do not cover every image pixel.

E4 reduces test $T$ from 0.014708 to 0.012364, or 15.93\%, while preserving contours close to the raw current frame. The improvement is smaller than the development reduction. E2 and E3 produce much smaller changes, so E4 is not the numerical winner. Its conservative weighting trades stronger smoothing for fidelity to current predictions. We have no video depth GT or calibrated lag measurement to establish that this trade-off is globally optimal.

Same-pixel EMA is less reliable under motion: its test $T$ increases by about 6.0\%, and moving rails leave visible repeated outlines. This reverses its development score improvement and illustrates why one temporal statistic is insufficient. Figure~\ref{fig:video} shows broadly similar structure from raw DA, E4, and VDA. VDA's separately normalized score is descriptive; neither its score nor its smoother appearance proves higher physical accuracy.

\subsection{Three observed difficult cases}
\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering\includegraphics[width=.92\textwidth]{images/difficult_cases.png}
\caption{Three real input conditions, with exact crops recorded in figure provenance. Top: development person at 3.000 s. Middle: test riders/rails at 1.458 s. Bottom: test distant detail at 2.875 s. E1 and E4 use the same selected weight and DA color limits; VDA uses its own limits. Crops preserve their aspect ratios.}
\label{fig:cases}
\end{figure*}
\paragraph{Moving foreground silhouette.}
At development frame 45, EMA visibly spreads the person's outline and leg region into nearby background. Flow-aligned E4 preserves a contour closer to the current raw prediction. Neither E4 nor VDA establishes the correct depth of the reflected appearance on the wet road. This is development evidence and is not counted as an additional independent test scene.

\paragraph{Riders crossing thin rails and supports.}
At test frame 35, small riders and intersecting supports overlap within a narrow image area. EMA produces strong repeated contours, whereas E4 keeps the raw outline more closely. The relative maps still merge much of the riders/track into smooth regions and do not reliably resolve every separation visible in RGB. Motion alignment cannot reconstruct missing detail from a coarse source prediction; disocclusion also removes a valid history correspondence.

\paragraph{Distant detail against sky.}
At test frame 69, the flagpole, small tower, and tree occupy a narrow band of relative values close to sky. Fine leaf structure and parts of the thin flagpole are weak in the displayed depth. E4 retains much of this limitation; it does not create recovered fine geometry. Fixed normalization makes the limited contrast visible, but without GT we cannot distinguish every true long-distance similarity from an erroneous flattening. These three cases are observed in the selected inputs rather than assumed examples of absent conditions.

\end{tabular}
\caption{Actual FP16 runs on the RTX 4060 Laptop. One complete run per setting; no repeated-run confidence interval. VDA uses temporal windows, while DA is frame-wise. Video output is $360\times640$; NYUv2 output is $480\times640$.}
\label{tab:cost}
\end{table*}
For the test clip, DA inference takes 5.124 s, bidirectional CPU flow takes 13.229 s, and E4 fusion takes 4.225 s. Their sum yields approximately 3.10 fps, slower than both raw DA and offline VDA. The CPU correspondence stage is the main additional cost. E4 is therefore a simple interpretable postprocessor, not a demonstrated speed improvement over the temporal model.

The 518-pixel VDA attempt failed during a padded warmup window with a requested allocation of about 4.27 GiB; this is retained as a resource failure, not excluded from the record. Successful 364-pixel inference peaks at 2.33 GiB allocated for both clips. These allocator measurements are useful for choosing hardware, but do not bound other programs' GPU use. Offline VDA throughput also cannot be equated with causal per-frame latency.
